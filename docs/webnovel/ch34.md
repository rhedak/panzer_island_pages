# Chapter 34: Both Things Are True

*Published August 24, 2026*

![Wilhelm and Erika facing each other across the central operations room of the research complex, his dark grey suit pale at every crease with the red tie loosened past holding a knot and exhaustion plain on him up close, her lab coat restored to full brightness, hands loose at her sides; between them the dormant Oracle console glowing a low warm amber, faintly echoed in his smartwatch; consoles along two walls, windows behind him with the Empire fleet holding position on the water, the intact maintained room in midday light](../assets/webnovel/ch34.webp){ .chapter-illustration }

The doors gave onto a corridor lit from above, evenly, the way a place lights itself when someone has kept paying for the power. My feet knew the turn before I saw it, left and then a short flight down, and I let them have it. Everything past this threshold was going to be mine before I remembered it as mine. I had stopped being surprised by that and had not stopped noticing it.

The air past the doors was warmer than the outer complex, and drier, and it smelled faintly of the same solvent-and-metal I remembered from every working lab I had stood in on this island whether I remembered standing in it or not. Someone had kept this building running the whole time. Someone had kept paying for it in every sense the word allows.

The corridor let out into a wide room with consoles along two walls and a bank of windows on the third, and Wilhelm was standing at the center console with his back to us. He turned when he heard us come in.

Behind me, Nadeshiko and Maria held the doorway without being told to. Neither of them said anything, which from Maria especially was its own kind of statement. I did not turn to check what was on either of their faces. Whatever it was, they were not going to hand it to me before I asked, and I did not ask.

He looked like himself. That was the strange part, that after everything the shape of him had not changed: tall, the same grey suit gone pale at every crease, the same red tie worked loose past the point of ever holding a knot again. Up close the thing the transmissions had never carried was plain on him. He had not slept in a way that showed. He was not troubling to hide it.

"You look like you," he said.

"You look angry."

"I have been angry for a long time."

"At me?"

He weighed it before he answered, visibly choosing the true one over the easy one. "At the situation. At Reiss. At myself. At you, yes. Not equally."

I let the room finish arriving before I used it. The console between us carried Oracle's status low and dormant, waiting on nothing I could see. Beyond him the windows gave onto the access road we had climbed and, past it, a grey line of water with shapes on it that did not move.

"The records in the government archive," I said. "Both our names were removed."

"I removed them."

He said it plainly, without asking to be forgiven for it first. "Reiss knew the committee had named investigators, and named investigators can be discredited. Records with no author attached are harder to suppress. They belong to no one who can be questioned." He let that stand a moment before he added the rest, the part he had clearly been saving. "I needed the evidence to survive long enough for you to find it."

I thought of the safety report with no name on it that had sent me across half the island before I understood whose warning I was following. I thought of an unsent order under a whiteboard with two clean signatures on it, and how long I had stood there before either of them meant anything to me. He had built a version of me a stranger could trust, out of a version of me he could not risk anyone identifying, and both of those had been the same decision made once.

I let him keep going, because I understood, standing there, that he had been keeping this in a particular order for a long time and I was not going to be the one who broke it.

"I also needed you to find your own work as a stranger. If you had found records with your name already on them, you might have defended what you found. Or denied it. Before you understood it. I needed you to read what someone built and follow it to the end." He looked at me instead of the console for this part. "And then find out the someone was you."

"And then find out," I said back to him, flat, because I did not have anywhere else to put it yet. I had walked the length of this island reading my own hand on a stranger's page, filing my own objections as another woman's caution, and he had built the road that way on purpose. Whether it was the right thing to have done to me was a question with no answer waiting behind it yet, and I set it beside the others I was keeping for later and went on.

"What is the purpose of this meeting?" Katyusha said, from behind me. Not hostile. She wanted the true shape of it and not the comfortable one, which was what she always wanted.

Wilhelm looked at her for a moment with something that was not warmth, closer to the respect he had not shown any of the units before this room. "She needs to decide what to do. That requires context. You have been supplying it since the beach." His eyes came back to me. "I have been supplying it longer. From a different direction." A short gesture, the console, the room, the whole quiet building around us. "This is where it resolves. Or where it begins to." A beat. "Depending."

Something changed in the doorway before either of us could go on. Nadeshiko went rigid. Maria's hand found the grip at her hip without being asked to. I turned enough to see what had put them there.

She was standing in the corridor we had just come up, filling it exactly to its width. Grey, still, set into the space like something built there: Alpha-Katyusha.

Wilhelm looked past us at her for exactly as long as it took to confirm what he had clearly already expected. "It has been doing that since you crossed the outer wall," he said, and went back to the console, the way a man goes back to work through weather he has already checked the forecast for.

Neither of them said anything to each other. That was the whole difference from the last time these two had stood across from each other: no argument, no accusation, nothing behind the grey eyes to answer. Only the fight. I stepped back against the console and stayed there, because a room this size gives you exactly one useful thing to do when you cannot fight, and that is to keep out of the lane. Nadeshiko and Maria held the doorway at her back so there was nowhere for it to go but forward, and Katyusha went to meet her own outline without a word, matching her stroke for stroke, two things built from the same design finding the same answers at the same instant, until one of them did not get up.

Katyusha stood over her a moment longer than the fight required, something working under the flat report that came out of her. "She has nothing to say."

"No," I said, and left it there.

"Sixty-one hours," Katyusha said, to no one in particular, already running the count again.

A sound reached us from somewhere outside, farther off than the door had been, closer than the perimeter. Drona, meeting whatever Oracle had sent to test the ground ahead of the fleet. Nobody in the room went to the windows for it. Wilhelm did not so much as turn his head, and I understood that he had already priced the sound in before we arrived.

"Reiss," he said. "You found his name in the northern archive. What the archive did not have was why a military commandant chaired a research committee, whose authority that served, what the arrangement actually was. The licensing agreement was negotiated over months and signed six months before the catastrophe. The evacuation order was timed to what they expected of you." He did not soften the next part, and I understood he had decided a long time ago not to. "They built the system to produce a specific outcome. Your activation was the anticipated outcome."

"So my activation prevented the transfer."

"Your activation caused the catastrophe. Both things are true." He said it without cruelty. It was a calculation he had clearly run about himself first, alone, more times over more years than he was going to tell me, and it showed in how little it cost him to say it now. "The system was built to exploit your decision. You made the decision you were engineered to make. The catastrophe followed. The handover did not. Both. At the same time."

I held that where I could look at it and waited for him to keep going, which he did, because he had more accounting than one room could carry in a single sitting and he knew it.

"Reiss is not on the fleet. He is not military. But the agreement he signed is on the fleet, and the people who mean to execute it are on the fleet, and they will use it whether or not you decide anything here today. We are not solving Reiss today. We are deciding what exists for that problem to attach to."

Something moved past the far edge of the windows, low and grey, one of Oracle's, and was gone before I had finished registering it. Nobody in the room broke stride for it either. This was simply what the room had become for him: a place where the fighting outside was a fact of weather, not an interruption.

"A question, not for the record." Katyusha did not turn from whatever she was tracking at the panel. "The engagement density since the western sector has been lower than this installation should have been able to produce. I flagged the discrepancy at the boundary. I have not closed it."

He did not pretend not to understand what she was actually asking. "I have had partial access to deployment scheduling since the reset. Not command. Access." He looked at the window, not at either of them. "I could not stop what was coming for you. I could space it. Smaller engagements, spread out, instead of what this installation could actually have thrown at four people crossing an island on foot." A pause longer than the sentence needed. "I do not know if that was mercy or only the last lever I had that still worked. I have not decided."

Nobody asked him to decide it faster.

"There is a second accounting," he said. "It does not belong to Reiss."

He came around the console to say it, the first time he had moved since we entered, and he looked at me instead of at anything else in the room. "The western sector. The deployment order. Both our names, clean signatures, ink. It was not Reiss who put those signatures there. Not the committee. Not the structure they built around us. We signed it." Something in him held there before he went on, longer than anything he had let land yet. "I have spent years trying to reconstruct why it was never sent. I cannot find the sequence. I do not know what stopped it."

"I don't remember signing it." The words were out before I had finished checking them, and nothing rose to meet the check. Not a pen, not a table, not his hand or mine moving across a page: only the flat fact of the order lying under a board a whole island away, and no road back to the hour that made it.

"I know. I was there." He did not make it gentler for either of us. "You signed it. I signed it. I should have refused. I signed it."

He let it stand a moment, then went on, careful now in a different register, the register of a man laying two objects on a table far enough apart that no one could mistake one for the other. "The Oracle accounting has Reiss in it. It has the committee in it. There are things to point to. The western sector, that accounting is ours. I am naming it separately so you do not carry it as the same thing." He said the last part slower than the rest. "It is not the same thing."

"The first transmission," he said, before I could answer that. "'All of it.' I had Reiss, the committee, all of it, when I sent that word. I said it that way because I needed you moving, and I was angry, and I chose to present it the way that would move you."

"It worked."

"Yes."

I let that sit between us the length of one breath. He had said what he came to say about it, and I could see in the set of him that he was not going to say more, that this was the shape of the apology he had decided he was entitled to make and no larger.

The fleet held its line beyond the glass, patient, and neither of us looked at it, though it was there the whole time, the condition the whole room was standing inside of. I thought of every version of him I had built out of a photograph and a voice on a bad line, careful, managing, three steps ahead of whatever I might feel. None of those versions had said *I should have refused.* None of them had let me watch him not know what stopped a signed order from going out. He had stopped managing me somewhere in this room, without announcing it, and I did not yet know what I was supposed to do with a version of him that had.
