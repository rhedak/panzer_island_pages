# Chapter 29: The Second Doctor

*Published August 12, 2026*

![Katyusha standing before Erika in a long institutional documentation room, hands empty at her sides, precise and formal as she delivers her disclosure, her orange side-braid and sky blue jacket the one strong color against rows of grey shelving; Erika, blonde ponytail and glasses, stands controlled and still at an open folder; Maria watches from a reading shelf in the middle distance, Nadeshiko further back between the shelves, late-afternoon light low through high small windows](../assets/webnovel/ch29.webp){ .chapter-illustration }

I woke with the day half spent and a navy jacket laid over me that was not mine. The light had crossed from one jamb of the corridor doorway to the other while I slept: early afternoon. Nobody had woken me. That made it a decision.

My body filed its report one item at a time while I sat up, better than any I had given it grounds for in a week. Katyusha handed me food before I was fully awake and then stood in front of me while I ate it, which in her grammar was a full sentence. I ate all of it. The corkboard was behind me, and I did not read it again. I folded Maria's jacket over the arm of the recliner, and we walked back to the junction, and this time I took the route Katyusha had logged.

The archive wing began where the working corridors stopped pretending to be anything else: a long spine of document rooms, one opening into the next, windows high and small, afternoon light going long through them. This was the oldest paper on the coast. The rooms near the entrance held the beginning of it, and the beginning was legitimate: soil chemistry, agent neutralization, protective doctrine, the science of keeping a population alive downwind of someone else's weapons. I read the earliest folders standing, and for the first hour I could almost believe there was nothing in the room to be ashamed of.

Then the scope changed, in a proposal with an approval attached, both signed. Then it changed again, the same way. I knew this line. I had read its middle stretch yesterday, in the records section, and understood it as a straight line from the first honest thing to the last. What the archive added was the first honest thing itself, and the arithmetic of the steps: each extension standing on the one before it, each well reasoned, defensive chemistry becoming the thing in the logistics room one signature at a time, no step anywhere in the sequence large enough to refuse.

Nadeshiko had been reading over my shoulder for three folders. At the second extension she stopped.

"When did it stop being defensive?"

"It never fully was." I kept my place in the folder and turned it toward her: the first proposal, the earliest and cleanest paper in the wing, and at the back of it an annex of offensive contingencies, typed in the same pass as the rest. "Even the first version was built to be used on people if it came to that. The protection was real. It was never the whole of it."

She took that and went back down the sequence the other way, oldest first, as if the paper might read differently in reverse. It would not. We had the whole of it, every step from this keeps people safe to a finished order with two signatures, and there was no gap left in it to put a better version of us in.

Katyusha, further along the room, had stopped at a shelf without taking anything from it. "These records have been handled. Recently, and in sequence." She tilted her head a degree. "Read and returned. One folder has been re-shelved two places out of sequence."

"Drona." Maria did not look up from the section she was working. "She reads everything that touches the thing she's putting together. She's been ahead of us in every room that mattered."

Maria's section was naval coordination. She had found it the way she found water, and read a binder there for a long time, not chemistry at all. When she finally spoke, it was a plain report.

"They weaponized the defense layer." She turned the binder a half turn so the diagram faced us. "The blockade coordination system. The thing built to keep threats off this coast, they modified it to carry the weapons inside it. Delivery through the picket network. My network."

Katyusha crossed to the binder and read the routing table once. "The station designators match the transmission log at the array." She looked at me. "And the vector section of the order."

"I know." It was my voice. Both of them looked at me. "I read the match yesterday, at the array, and said nothing." I had spent a day and a night deciding that was mine to hold, and standing in the room where the modification had been filed I could not reconstruct why. "I am saying it now."

Maria looked at me a moment longer than the admission needed. Then she looked back down at the binder.

"My kind," she reported, in the same easy register she would have used for a weather change. "I was built from the framework that did this. Not this exactly. The same design lineage." She closed the binder without care and without contempt, an operational close, a thing finished being read. "I keep thinking that should make me feel something specific. I haven't worked out what yet."

None of us tried to help her with it. There was no help that would not have been a lie.

---

*Katyusha*

The author column repeated two names down the length of the documentation room, and I had stopped reading for content an hour before. I was reading for density now: how often the second name appeared deciding, approving, signing. The count had been high since the administration section, and it no longer changed anything.

During the second watch of the rest period, while the doctor slept, I had re-run the voice identification against yesterday's transmission. It did not require a third confirmation. The match from the Nest had been complete for nine days. I ran it anyway and logged the confirmation.

In the documentation room, between an authorization and its extension, I queried the restriction on the record behind it, on a schedule I had stopped classifying as maintenance some sectors ago.

The query returned open.

My hands had stopped over the folder. I logged the stop. It had lasted one and one-tenth seconds and served no coverage purpose I could name, and I verified the return twice. The record stood open, and appended to it was the restriction's release condition. The condition had never been in my records to read. It belonged to the lock, not to me. Not a date. Not a location. A knowledge state, and the state was the doctor's.

He had said it to her through the receiver as if it were his own thought. Remember it. Not be told it. The condition in my record was older than his transmission, and it was the same design. I filed the correspondence. I did not have a classification for what the filing displaced.

I had maintained a disclosure statement since the Nest. With the restriction open I read it over once. Every sentence was accurate, and the statement was wrong. It had been drafted to deliver an identification that was no longer news; the team had carried his name since the Nest. What the record held that the doctor did not was smaller than a name and heavier. The assignment. The nine days. There was no statement format for either.

I deleted the draft, and crossed the documentation room to where she stood.

---

*Erika*

Katyusha stopped in front of me with her hands empty. She did not begin with a status report, and the last time she hadn't, it was outside the archive under the pines, with the same word waiting behind her teeth.

"Doctor. The word Doctor is a title in my training records. It designates someone under my protection. I told you at the archive: the assignment covers two individuals. I could not give you the second identity then." Nothing in her posture moved. "The restriction on that record has released. I found it open this afternoon. The second individual is Wilhelm."

The room went quiet in the way a room does when something lands that everyone has half built a place for. Two Doctors on every document in this wing. Two names on the assignment. I had known the shape and left the space empty, because filling it meant this: the unit that had walked at my shoulder the length of this island had been built to protect both of the people who made all of it, and one of them was me, and the other was setting our route.

"How long have you known?"

"The identification completed at the Nest. I ran a voice match against my training records during the NEXUS engagement. It completed. The record behind it was locked." Then, so that I would not have to do the subtraction myself: "Nine days."

"You matched him in the middle of the fight." Nadeshiko's voice had gone up a register. "In the middle of it, and then you just..." The sentence restarted somewhere else. "You were built to protect him. Him."

"Yes. During that engagement I widened my coverage arc eleven degrees toward the north end of the building, where his transmissions originated. I logged the adjustment as a formation correction. The entry was not accurate. Both of my assignments were inside that building." She turned her head to me. "I amended the entry this afternoon. You may read it."

"Why now?" I asked it evenly. "Why did it release now?"

"The restriction carried its own release condition. The condition belonged to the lock, not to my records. I received it only when the lock opened." She recited it the way she recites coordinates. "A knowledge state. Yours, Doctor. The record was permitted to open once you had recovered, yourself, the facts it stood on. Both names. The program. What the two of you built into yourselves." A beat. "You recovered the last of them this morning. In the room where you slept. This afternoon I ran my usual query, the first since before the suite."

I stood with that longer than I let show. Yesterday, through a receiver, he had told me I needed to remember it and not be told it, and I had heard the sentence as his philosophy, his design for this whole punitive curriculum of a coastline. It was on record as mine first. I had built the same rule into her, before the catastrophe, in the same hand that signed the extensions in this room: nothing may be handed to me that I have not already carried in myself. Two architects, one doctrine, and I could no longer tell which of us had taught it to the other.

"You have held this since the Nest."

"Longer." For once, the precision did not arrive immediately, and I did not hurry it. "The assignment has been active since I first came online. The Nest gave it its second name."

"Are you angry?" Nadeshiko asked it before I could decide whether I was allowed to. "You get to be, you know."

"I have logged an irritation state at intervals since the relay station. Logging has not resolved it." Katyusha considered. "That is the complete report."

Maria laughed, once, low, and nobody begrudged her it.

"The other restrictions hold," Katyusha added, to me, before I could put my next question anywhere. "This one released. The rest have not. Their conditions are their own."

I looked at her and finally put together the two things I had been keeping apart. She had carried an assignment naming the man we were walking toward, locked behind a condition I wrote, and she had walked the coast beside me without letting the weight show, and the person who had done that to her was the same one she was built to protect. An apology would have been for my own benefit, and she would have logged it as exactly that.

"Thank you for carrying it," I said instead, and Katyusha inclined her head one degree, and that transaction was complete and accurate on both sides.

The quiet that followed had a question standing in it, and I turned to where Maria was leaning at the reading shelf. She knew what I was going to ask before my head finished turning, and she did not make me ask it into a joke.

"And yours."

She came off the lean. No tilt, no performance, hands still, her eyes level on mine, the drop arriving before the answer did so that I would know exactly what the answer was made of.

"Not yet, Doc. Almost."

I believed her. Whatever measure she had been keeping, it had just watched a restriction release and a doctrine get named, and it had still come back almost. The measure was not mine to hurry. I nodded, and she let the performance settle back over her like the jacket she had put over me while I slept.

The exit from the archive wing was a single corridor at its far end, and Alpha-Maria was standing in it.

Facing us, not at a shelf or over a folder: standing in the corridor the way a final obstacle stands, nothing in her hands for the first time since the tide-mark. She was not looking at the team. She was looking at Maria.

Katyusha moved first, unhurried, already decided, and at the moment her advance would have become an engagement, Alpha-Maria turned and walked through the exit door, unhurried, no glance back. The door stood open behind her, and by the time Katyusha reached it, the corridor was empty.

"She has been leaving before we arrive," Katyusha noted. "For every sector."

"I noticed."

Maria did not say when she started noticing, and nobody asked.

Beyond the exit door the complex opened into a service gallery, and Drona was already in it, walking.

Not toward us, not away: crossing the gallery on her own line, in the strict, spent economy her movement had come down to since the school. She reached the cross-corridor Alpha-Maria had taken and did not stop at it. She gave it one look in passing, the look you give a thing already measured, and kept walking, and both of her headphone earpieces were lit a steady orange, no flicker, the whole time she crossed our field of view.

She went on down the gallery, deeper in, the direction we were going anyway, and did not look back.

Neither did we.
