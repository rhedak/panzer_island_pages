# Chapter 37: The Others Arrive

*Published September 1, 2026*

![Alpha-Maria, a greyscale mirror of a navy-jacketed ship officer with a captain's hat worn level and unstyled, in close foreground mid-sentence in an institutional corridor, facing camera with total stillness; behind and beside her, Alpha-Katyusha and Alpha-Nadeshiko just arrived, greyscale against the grey-blue institutional walls, present rather than posed; in the middle distance, softly lit, Erika (blonde ponytail, glasses, lab coat) and her team Katyusha, Nadeshiko, Maria and Wilhelm face the three arrivals; the corridor recedes toward Oracle's dormant console, a single warm amber glow at the far end echoed by the small warm light of Wilhelm's smartwatch; a window along the corridor's side shows the reflecting pool and the Empire's fleet holding position on a pale horizon; quiet, converged, decisive](../assets/webnovel/ch37.webp){ .chapter-illustration }

The corridor past the operations room was darker than the room we had left and had not warmed at all in the time it took us to notice it. Institutional grey, the same flat ceiling strips as every other stretch of this complex, a smell of cold concrete and something faintly metallic underneath it that none of us had bothered to name. It also, within the first few steps, stopped feeling empty.

Nobody said it aloud, but I watched all three of them notice it, each in her own manner: Katyusha's weight shifting forward onto the balls of her feet before her hands found anything to justify it, Nadeshiko's head turning half a degree toward a doorway none of us had reached yet, Maria going quiet in the specific way she goes quiet when she has already decided something and is waiting for the rest of us to catch up. They were not converging on us. They were converging on the building, the way water finds the low ground without anyone directing it there, because the building was where Oracle was, and Oracle was what they were made of, and none of that had ever stopped being true just because the reset stopped them from remembering it.

I did not need to be told which corridor held which of them. My feet had already turned toward the first one.

Alpha-Katyusha was standing outside the operations room door, in the middle of the corridor, at a distance that was neither approach nor retreat. Nothing about how she stood asked to be noticed and nothing about it hid. When we came into her sightline she looked at Katyusha, then at me, then back at Katyusha, and settled there, still as a scale that had already balanced.

"You reached the testing range before us," Katyusha said.

"I reached this center before you as well."

"You have been following the same path we have."

"I have been following the path that led here. You have been following the path that led here." Nothing in how she said it asked for agreement. "They are not the same path."

"They arrive at the same point."

"Yes." There was no charge in how Katyusha said it, only confirmation. "I find that significant."

"So do I."

Neither of them moved to close the remaining distance. I thought of the last two times these two had been in a room together, if room is the right word for a firing range and a loading bay: Alpha-Katyusha a shape at the far end of a line of markers, unhurried and unbothered by being seen; then, across a confrontation space, telling Katyusha flatly that knowing the cover positions was not the same thing as knowing what happened here. None of that was in this corridor. It simply stood, agreed upon, between two people built from the same design.

Alpha-Katyusha turned first, unhurried, and let the corridor take her toward wherever she had been going before we arrived. Katyusha watched her go the length of it and did not follow. The corridor held the quiet a moment longer, then let it go, like a held breath once its reason has passed.

We found the next one two doors down, in a small administrative office off the main corridor, the kind of room built for a person who managed paperwork for someone more important than themselves: a desk shoved to one wall, a filing cabinet with its top drawer swollen shut, a window that had probably once looked out on nothing more urgent than a supply yard.

Maria glanced in after us. "Middle management," she said, low, to nobody. "Rest in peace." Nobody laughed, which did not appear to bother her at all.

Alpha-Nadeshiko was at that window when Nadeshiko found her, facing a view that ran north over the complex wall toward country none of us could see from here but all of us knew the shape of by now: the winter streets, the plaza, the terrace, the three hundred and forty-two names weighted under a stone against the wind. She heard the room fill and turned without startling.

"You are not angry," she said.

"I'm still angry." Nadeshiko crossed half the room and stopped there, not closing it further. "At the system. Not at you."

"I do not fully understand the distinction."

"I know." Something in Nadeshiko's voice that was almost warmth and almost its opposite, holding both at once. "That's the distinction."

Alpha-Nadeshiko looked back at the window, at the sector she had spent an entire arc defending in a register none of us had access to until we walked through it ourselves. "The sector is intact," she said. "The records are maintained."

"I know. I read some of them."

"Then you know what was there."

"Yes." Nadeshiko held her stare. "It was worth cataloguing."

The pause that followed ran longer than the ones I had watched her spend on flat facts before, across a plaza and an observation terrace and now this narrow office.

"Yes," she said, finally. "It was."

Nadeshiko heard it land the same way I did. Neither of them said anything about that part, and neither did I.

Katyusha caught up to us in the doorway and said nothing, which from her was its own kind of acknowledgment. We left Alpha-Nadeshiko at her window and went on toward the main corridor, the one that ran straight to the outer entrance and, past it, to whatever position Drona had chosen for herself.

Maria hadn't said anything else since the office. She found her the same way anyone finds something carried the entire distance and set down exactly once, a long time ago, in a place still reachable without needing to look for the route.

Alpha-Maria was holding the center of the main corridor, between the outer entrance and the room we had just left, in a stance that was not defensive and was not an invitation either. When Maria came into range she did not step back and she did not step forward. She held the ground she had already chosen, exactly as she had held the boundary line at the edge of the western contamination the last time these two had stood this close to each other.

"The ledger," Maria said. "Did you close it?"

"Which entry?"

"The one you couldn't close. Whether I'd have done better."

"No."

Maria took the flat no straight on, and kept going. "I'd have tried. Probably not been enough. Tried anyway."

"I remember."

"Is that enough?"

Alpha-Maria's quiet, across every one of these near-encounters that never quite became fights, had stopped reading to me as avoidance.

"I do not know," she said. "I think it may be the most honest answer that entry admits of."

"Yeah," Maria said, and that was all either of them had for it.

Behind Alpha-Maria, the corridor's far end filled without ceremony. Alpha-Katyusha first, then Alpha-Nadeshiko a few steps behind her, arriving as three people arrive at a conversation that has already moved somewhere new: not summoned, not coordinated, simply present because the subject had reached the part that concerned all of them. They did not form up. They stood as each of them had already been standing alone, only now in the same room, and faced us.

Wilhelm had said nothing since the operations room, and he said nothing now, but I watched him watch the three of them arrange themselves without his instruction and understood what that cost him: Katyusha had told him this, and Drona had told him this before her, and here it was standing in front of him anyway, no longer needing to be told.

"You are about to decide what Oracle becomes," Alpha-Maria said, to all of us and none of us in particular, in the same register her counterpart uses on a room she already knows will listen. "We are part of that."

Beside me, Katyusha's eyes stayed on Alpha-Maria, not on me. She had told me, weeks ago now, that a piece of her own design sat somewhere inside Oracle's architecture and had not been able to finish the sentence that followed. I did not think she had finished it yet. I thought she was listening to see whether Alpha-Maria could.

On my other side, Nadeshiko had gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the earlier office. She was not looking at Alpha-Maria. She was looking at Alpha-Nadeshiko, who had not spoken since arriving, and who was looking back.

"Whatever you decide about Oracle, you are also deciding about us. We have been pointing toward this moment since the testing grounds. You should have that information."

Nobody answered her. It was not a question, and I understood by now that none of the three of them asked questions they expected an answer to change. I reached, in the space that left, for something to hold the weight of what she had just handed me, a rule or a precedent or anything my own hands might already know the shape of. Nothing arrived. Whatever this decision was going to run on, it was not going to be something I already had.

Into the silence, Alpha-Nadeshiko said, "I maintained three hundred and forty-two names." Flat, exact, the same register she had used at a plaque and at a terrace and would, I suspected, use for the rest of whatever she had left to be. "I still maintain it."

Nadeshiko did not flinch at hearing her own number spoken back to her by the one person who had kept it longer. Wilhelm, beside me, closed his eyes for the length of one breath and opened them again, and did not otherwise move. He had assigned that unit her position at this center believing he understood what he was setting in motion. I did not think he had expected her to still be counting.

I had no answer ready for that either, and before I found one the building answered for me. Sound reached us from outside, past the outer entrance, past whatever ground Drona had claimed for herself: a low, sustained percussion working its way through the walls, distinct from anything the complex's own systems had made since we arrived. The unmistakable weight of engagement starting somewhere it had not been supposed to start yet: the Empire's advance element, earlier than we had been told to expect it, and Drona already in it.

The three grayscale figures in front of us turned toward the sound in the same half-second, before any of us had finished understanding what it was. Katyusha moved for the entrance before I had finished the thought that she would; Nadeshiko a step behind her, one hand rising to something at her ear that was not there yet. Whatever this room had been about to become, it was not going to finish becoming it in here.
