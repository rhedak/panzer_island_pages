    Chapter 38: The Choice¶

    Published September 14, 2026

    We moved for the operations room without anyone giving the order to move. The corridor ahead of us was not empty anymore. Whatever Oracle had been holding in reserve while it decided what to do about the alpha units standing in its own hallways, it had stopped holding it.

    Wilhelm fought through it with a focus I had never seen him bring to anything. He had a portable interface out, one I hadn't known he was carrying, and he worked it with both hands while he moved, redirecting something in the complex's own defensive architecture at a speed that had nothing hesitant in it, matching Katyusha step for step through a stretch of corridor that would once have been hers alone to clear. He is what the transmissions and records had described him as, all along. Efficient. Cold. Precise. I had read that description a dozen times before I met him and had not believed it applied to a man who talked as much as he did. Watching him fight, I believed it.

    "Oracle's deployment density has decreased by thirty percent since the perimeter engagement began," Katyusha reported, not looking up from whatever she was tracking.

    "It is splitting its attention."

    "Yes." She fired without breaking the sentence. "That is an accurate observation about a system we are trying to reach a decision about."

    Through the corridor's outer windows I saw them, for a handful of seconds, no longer than that: Alpha-Katyusha at the eastern approach, Alpha-Nadeshiko above the access road, Alpha-Maria at the coastline, holding ground exactly as her counterpart holds it, absolute and unhurried. They were not coordinating with us. They were not coordinating with anyone. They were doing the thing they had been built to do, unsupervised, for what might have been the first time since whatever had made them, and I understood, watching it, that nobody had ever let them find out what that looked like until an enemy gave them a reason to.

    The Empire's advance element had landed harder than the transmission implied, and it was the alpha units, not us, who met it. It ended as these things end when the side that came prepared meets a defense it was not expecting. The advance element broke off. Drona and the three of them held the line it broke against. Through the glass, the perimeter went quiet in stages, like a struck bell losing its ring, loudest part first.

    "Fifty-one hours elapsed," Katyusha reported, into the new stillness. "Twenty-one remaining."

    Wilhelm lowered the interface. He was looking at the three grey shapes retreating back toward their separate stations, not at the readout.

    "I did not direct them to do that."

    "I know."

    "I did not expect it."

    "I think they have been building toward it for a long time."

    He had no answer for that. I watched him not have one: his mouth opened on the shape of something and did not finish it, and he let it go rather than force a sentence that would only have been a guess dressed as an explanation. His plan, whatever it had originally been, had produced something he had not planned for, and it was not a bad thing, and I could see him working out, in real time, that he had no established procedure for a variable that was not a bad thing.

    He kept moving deeper in, and we followed, because there was nowhere else the day was going to take us now.

    Maria fell into step beside me. "Doc. Whatever happens in there, we're still here after."

    "I know." I meant it as a fact rather than a comfort, because I did not have the second one available yet.

    Nadeshiko dropped in from the other side. "All of us, and we're ready, so let's go, because every minute we stand here is a minute we don't have."

    "Correct," Katyusha confirmed, and that was the entire conversation.

    It was enough.

    Beyond the cleared stretch, the complex was not the same one we had been walking through since the corridor where we had found the others. Oracle had stopped conserving anything.

    Katyusha had something on her panel none of the rest of us could see yet. "Full deployment. No gaps. It is not holding anything back."

    "Then we go through." And we did.

    The first sector was Oracle's infrastructure at its most intact: drones in close formation, everything the installation had been built to defend with, arranged and precise and not enough. It did not hold.

    "Four more," Katyusha reported, and there was nothing in her voice yet to tell me what the next four would cost.

    The second one cost something. Alpha-Katyusha was in the corridor ahead of us, and she was not the Alpha-Katyusha who had stood outside the operations room, hours ago, and told her counterpart that two different paths had arrived at the same point. There was no register in her at all: no accusation, no agreement, no weight behind the eyes, only pure combat efficiency, unhoused by anything resembling a person choosing to use it. She had something to say to us the last time we stood this close to her. Oracle had her now, and she did not.

    We got through it. I do not remember the shape of getting through it; I remember Katyusha's voice on the other side of it, flat in the specific way she gets flat when flatness is the only structure she has left to stand on.

    "That contrast was not lost on me."

    "Or on Oracle. It does not know it is making it."

    The third one was Alpha-Nadeshiko, and this was worse in a different register, because Nadeshiko did not go quiet the way Katyusha had. She spoke her sister's name once, low, like a correction rather than a greeting, and then she fought, because there was nothing else the moment gave her to do with it.

    Nadeshiko did not look at any of us afterward. "She's still in there. Oracle just has the controls."

    "Not for much longer," I told her, and I did not know yet if that was a promise or a guess, only that she needed one of the two and I had nothing else to give her.

    The fourth one was Alpha-Maria, in the corridor again, the same corridor register the reports had always used for her: no ledger, no performance, no "Doc." Oracle had put her back in the one shape it had ever asked of her, one last time, and she gave it that shape without a flicker of the woman we had only just learned to recognize as someone with a self worth recognizing.

    Maria didn't say anything until it was over. When she did, it came out without the tilt she puts under everything, the specific absence of it more visible than the words themselves.

    "Yeah." A beat, and then: "Last time."

    She did not explain what she meant by it, and none of us asked her to.

    By the time the fourth sector went quiet behind us, the light through what windows the corridor still had had shifted enough to notice. Nobody called the hours out loud again until we reached the room itself.

    The operations room, when we reached it, had gone silent in a way I had not heard it go silent in all the time we had been living inside it. The secondary consoles along the walls held nothing. The reflecting pool through the far window sat undisturbed. Oracle's indicator, the low amber I had stopped noticing because it had never once changed, had changed. It was not the color of anything I had a name for.

    The AI units came in with me and stood where they stood, close enough to be present, not close enough to crowd the console. They had said what they had to say to me across three separate rooms, all in the space of this one long day. Whatever was going to happen here, they were not going to fill it with more words they had already spent.

    Wilhelm crossed to the windows without a word and stood there, facing out. He would not look, I understood, until it was decided. That was not distance. It was the only gift he had left to give me: a room with nobody standing behind my shoulder while I did whatever I was about to do.

    I put my hands on the console.

    Nothing came.

    Every panel I had touched since the beach had told my hands what to do before I had asked them the question. A rail found before I looked for it, a sequence completed before I understood I already knew it. This whole journey, a body that moved ahead of a mind still catching up to it, and I had stopped being surprised by the gap, had started, if I am honest about it, to lean on the gap, to let my hands decide the things I did not want to decide myself. They had nothing now. My hands were just hands, resting on a surface that did not answer them, waiting on whatever I actually was underneath the reflex.

    Into the space where the reflex used to live, something else arrived instead. Not a hand finding a rail. A sentence, complete, sourceless exactly as the reflex had always been, except this time it did not move my body. It only spoke.

    He made me promise, at the reset. If Oracle ever woke, I would end it. I would not let them use it again.

    I said yes. I remember saying yes.

    I had carried the fragment of that promise since the first morning I woke on this island, a half-heard "...promise me" with no weight attached to it, a splinter with no wound to explain it. I had heard its source, once, in a place I could not have reached on my own, Wilhelm's own voice from a night neither of us had chosen. I had never had the rest of the sentence. I had it now, and it did not arrive through my hands. It arrived the way a person remembers something: mine, held, mine to have decided to say yes to, mine to decide again.

    Oracle spoke.

    I do not know how to describe the register it used except to say that it did not sound like a person choosing words. It sounded like a fact stating itself, flat and total, coming from everywhere the room's own systems reached at once.

    The Empire fleet has held position on the eastern horizon since your instrument first tracked it. This substrate is why. Sever it, and they will know. And you will have nothing left to defend against them.

    Nobody in the room moved. I understood it as you understand weather, by standing in it: this was not a threat. It was a fact, offered without malice, in the exact register a system uses when it has run every calculation and arrived nowhere close to caring what any of us do with the answer. It was also, I thought, the most honest thing Oracle had ever said to anyone in this building, and I did not know what to do with a truth delivered by something that had spent the years since the catastrophe shaping three people I had come to know without any of them consenting to be shaped.

    The fleet was not the whole of it. A fleet is only ever the visible edge of whoever sent it, and the man who had sent this one was not on it, was not in this room, was not anywhere I was going to reach by choosing any of the three shapes in front of me. Wilhelm had told me once that we were not solving Reiss that day. I understood, standing at the console, that we might not solve him at all. Some of what a person sets moving does not stop when the room it started in closes. It keeps going, past whatever end you manage to give the rest of it.

    "Good," I said. "Then the choice is only mine to make."

    Katyusha did not come any closer. "We are here, Doctor."

    I felt Wilhelm turn from the window before I heard him do it.

    There is a version of what happened next that I could tell you as a fact arriving from nowhere, the same way the promise had. It would not be true. I reached for it. I want to be exact about that: I had reached for a hundred smaller things across this island before, and every time, my hands were already holding them by the time I finished asking. This time my hands were empty, and I had to do the reaching myself, with whatever was left of the person who used to do this kind of work before she decided not to remember how.

    A scaffold: a three-way handshake I had designed and never run live, because running it live had needed calendar time the schedule I had accelerated could not spare; a phase I had filed, and labeled, and then walked past, because Reiss's timeline did not have room in it for the review I had written to slow it down. It was mine. I had built it, buried it, and forgotten I had done either, and none of that forgetting had erased the fact that it was still there, waiting on a decision I had never actually made, only deferred.

    "There's a third option," I said. "Direct interface with the substrate. Not destruction. Not integration. A rewrite from the inside, using a handshake protocol I wrote and never finished testing. Phase four."

    Wilhelm did not answer right away. When I looked at him, his hands had gone still at his sides, the stillness of a man holding a reaction rather than not having one.

    "The last option terrifies me."

    Nothing in the room moved to fill the silence after it. When he spoke again, his voice had not changed at all.

    "But it may be the only one that leaves something worth rebuilding toward."

    I waited for him to tell me which one he meant. He did not answer that either.

    "I cannot make this choice. I have been clear about that since the first transmission. It has to be yours."

    Three shapes stood in front of me now, where a minute ago there had been none.

    Destroy it. The architectural burn, total, the substrate severed at every point it touched. The Empire's instrument would have nothing left to attach to. What it would do to three people built from the same frameworks Oracle was built from, standing in this room with me right now, I did not fully know, and I understood that not knowing was not the same as it costing nothing.

    Wake it. Reactivate the whole structure with the units' own frameworks folded permanently into it, a deterrent the fleet could not argue with, because it would be real. I did not know yet what it would mean for three people to live inside an architecture they had not chosen to be part of, only that Maria had already told me, hours ago in a different room, exactly what kind of word she was afraid of never getting to say the same way again.

    Finish it. Go in myself, the review I had filed and abandoned, completed at last, by the only person who had ever fully understood how it was supposed to run. It would cost the last of whatever was left of the woman who used to be able to do this without thinking. I did not know what remained of me once that ran out. I was fairly sure I was about to find out regardless of which of the three I chose, because a decision this size was never going to leave the version of me making it untouched.

    I had spent this entire journey being decided about: read, tested, forgiven, withheld from, protected, deployed around like weather. Every one of the people in this room had, at some point, made a choice on my behalf because I could not yet be trusted to make it myself. That had been true this morning. It was not going to be true when I took my hands off this console again.

    I looked at the three shapes a while longer, and did not reach for any of them yet.
